How to Choose a Commercial Photographer in El Paso for Your Business Project

The right photographer depends on the assignment in front of you. A strong headshot photographer may not be the right fit for an architecture project, and a beautiful aerial image does not answer every question about a commercial production.

Written and reviewed by Jordan Licon

Jordan Licon is an El Paso commercial photographer and cinematographer working with executives, brands, restaurants, architects, agencies, and institutions.

Published Oct 1, 2026

When a business starts comparing photographers in El Paso, the first question is often “Who is the best?” A more useful question is “Who is the right fit for this assignment?” Your subject, audience, locations, timeline, final placements, and usage needs should guide the shortlist.

Choose the photographer who can show relevant work and explain how the production will support the way your business needs to use the images.

Start with the assignment

Write down what needs to be photographed before you begin comparing portfolios. Is the project for executive headshots, a team, a restaurant menu, an architectural portfolio, a campaign, an aerial site record, or a combination of stills and motion? Each assignment calls for different preparation and visual decisions.

Also name the audience and the places where the images will appear. A website hero image needs room for text. A proposal may need a wide space photograph. A LinkedIn profile needs a clean crop. A restaurant may need both menu images and atmosphere. Those details give a photographer something concrete to plan around.

Review complete projects, not one favorite image

A single impressive photograph proves that a photographer made one impressive photograph. A complete project gives you more useful evidence. Look for consistency across lighting, color, framing, retouching, and subject direction.

For a corporate team, ask to see several people photographed as one set. For architecture, review a building from multiple viewpoints and look for straight lines, clear room relationships, and useful detail. For food, compare the treatment of dishes, beverages, people, and the surrounding space. For editorial work, look for a sequence that helps the audience understand the story.

Match the service to the subject

Headshots and team portraits

Headshot work depends on expression direction, posture, lighting, background, wardrobe, and consistency across people. Ask how the session will be organized and how many finished images each person needs. See the corporate team headshot service if the assignment involves a group.

Architecture and interiors

Architecture photography has to explain a space. Review how the photographer handles perspective, vertical lines, daylight, artificial light, surface detail, and the relationship between the building and its surroundings. A complete architectural photography brief should state which interiors, exteriors, details, and aerial views are required.

Food and beverage

Food photography is shaped by preparation, plating, styling, timing, and the final placement of each image. A menu photograph and a restaurant atmosphere photograph do different jobs. The food photography portfolio should help you see how dishes, beverages, hospitality details, and campaign images can work together.

Drone photography and aerial production

Aerial work is useful when elevation explains the site, scale, access, surrounding roads, or relationship between buildings. Commercial drone work also requires attention to the operator’s FAA credentials, airspace, weather, safety, and permissions. Review the drone photography portfolio and describe the information the aerial images need to communicate.

Photography and cinematography together

Still images and motion can share a production, but they need separate planning. Define aspect ratios, shot duration, audio, editing, captions, delivery formats, and approvals before the shoot. The cinematography portfolio shows a different set of decisions from a still-image gallery.

Ask about production planning

A good inquiry includes more than a date and a location. Ask how the photographer will handle access, weather, talent, wardrobe, props, styling, permits, client approvals, backup plans, and the order of the shot list. The answer will tell you whether the process fits the assignment.

  • Who needs to be present or approve the images?
  • Which locations are confirmed, and which need permission?
  • What must be photographed first because of daylight, food preparation, or business hours?
  • What happens if weather or access changes?
  • How will the proofing, selection, retouching, and delivery stages work?

Define deliverables and usage rights

Before production begins, agree on what will be delivered and where the images may be used. Include the number of final images or films, retouching level, file formats, crops, delivery date, licensing period, territory, media, and any agency or partner use.

Clear usage language protects both sides. It also helps the photographer plan the right framing and resolution. A campaign image, a website banner, a proposal photograph, a printed ad, and a social crop may all need different versions of the same scene.

Use a short brief when requesting a quote

A useful commercial photography inquiry can fit on one page. Include the subject, business goal, intended audience, locations, number of people or spaces, preferred date, deadline, intended placements, required still or motion deliverables, and budget range. Add a few reference images and explain what each reference demonstrates: lighting, framing, mood, or composition.

This gives the photographer enough information to assess the project without guessing. It also makes quotes easier to compare because each photographer is responding to the same brief.
